How the model works, and where it stops

Stressed volume = max(0, total volume − unstressed capacity). Pmsf = stressed volume / venous compliance. Venous return = max(0, (Pmsf − RAP) / RVR). The heart is two pumps in series. Each ventricle has a straight end-systolic pressure–volume line (slope Ees) and a curved end-diastolic one. For a given flow, the LV ejects its stroke volume against roughly MAP plus any aortic valve gradient. That sets its end-systolic volume; adding the stroke volume gives end-diastolic volume, and the diastolic curve turns that into LVEDP. The RV does the same against PA pressure (mPAP = LA pressure + CO × PVR), and with TR it also has to pump the volume that leaks back. The model finds the flow at which the RAP the veins leave behind equals the RAP the right heart needs. A faster rate leaves less time to fill, so near normal filling most of the gain from rate cancels out. The model doesn't simulate pulsatile chamber pressures. When fluid, blood or relief of an obstruction brings flow back, HR, arteriolar tone and venoconstriction ease back toward the patient's own baseline, the way the baroreflex would. This doesn't reverse vasoplegia.

Ventricles and valves: When RV diastolic pressure exceeds LV diastolic pressure, the septum bows left (4 mL per mmHg of gradient), and the LV needs a higher pressure to reach the same volume. The pericardium holds both ventricles. Past about 300 mL combined it adds pressure to both, and over months it stretches to fit a chronically enlarged heart. Aortic stenosis uses the Gorlin relation: mean gradient = (SV / (ejection time × 44.3 × AVA))², so the gradient rises with the square of flow. Functional TR grows as the RV dilates and its pressure climbs.

Left-sided lesions: In HOCM the outflow gradient depends on how small the LV is in mid-systole. As the cavity shrinks the mitral leaflet is drawn into the outflow tract, so the gradient rises steeply with less volume, a lower MAP, more contractility and a faster rate, and the leaflet also leaks (SAM-related MR). The thick LV relaxes slowly, so a fast rate costs it more filling than it costs a normal heart. Mitral stenosis uses the Gorlin relation for diastolic flow: mean gradient = (LV filling per beat / diastolic filling time / (37.7 × MVA))². Diastole shortens as the rate rises, so a faster rate raises LA pressure sharply, and PCWP sits above LVEDP by that gradient. In MR the LV ejects its forward stroke volume plus a regurgitant volume back into the LA. The regurgitant fraction grows with the pressure the LV ejects against and with LV dilation, and the v-wave adds to mean LA pressure. Lowering SVR shrinks the leak and pushing SVR up widens it.

Restrictive and constrictive filling: In cardiac amyloid both ventricles get stiffer diastolic curves and fill almost entirely in early diastole, so a longer diastole adds little volume and output tracks heart rate. Contractility and reflex reserve are also reduced. In constrictive pericarditis the pericardium can't stretch over time; its pressure climbs steeply once the two ventricles together pass about 190 mL. That pressure adds to every chamber's diastolic pressure, which is why RAP, RVEDP, PA diastolic and PCWP come out close together. The ventricles eject against transmural pressure, so pericardial pressure is subtracted from systolic load too.

Cirrhosis and blunted compensation: Cirrhosis starts with dilated splanchnic vessels: low SVR, high output, and a large blood volume, much of it in unstressed capacity. Its vessels also respond less to the reflex. Blunted compensation models a beta-blocker or autonomic failure: when an acute insult would normally raise HR, arteriolar tone and contractility, this patient recruits only about a third of that rise. Neither changes the insult itself, only how much reflex the patient has to meet it.

Rhythm: In sinus rhythm the atrium tops up each ventricle at the end of diastole. Without that kick, passive filling has to deliver the whole volume, so the ventricle needs a higher pressure to reach the same end-diastolic volume. How much that costs depends on the ventricle. A stiff or slow-relaxing one (HFpEF, HOCM, amyloid, aortic stenosis) fills less early and leans on the atrium more, and its steep curve makes the extra volume expensive: losing the kick costs it about a fifth to a quarter of its output at rest. A compliant ventricle loses only a few percent. The atrial share grows with rate, because a fast rate cuts early filling short. In mitral stenosis the atrial push also drives flow across the valve, so losing it raises the gradient. AF with RVR sets the ventricular rate near 145 and removes the kick. That rate comes from the AV node, not the sinus node, so the baroreflex can't slow it as flow comes back. Complete heart block sets the rate near 38 with the atria beating out of step, so about half the kick is lost. A heart that can't fill more in a longer diastole loses output with the rate. Cardioversion restores the kick and hands the rate back to the sinus node. Permanent AF removes the kick for good. The model treats AF as a fast regular rate with no kick; it doesn't simulate beat-to-beat irregularity.

Procedures without an indication have their usual complication. A pericardiocentesis with no tamponade is a dry tap into the RV, and the blood it lets into the pericardium causes an iatrogenic tamponade. A needle decompression with no tension pneumothorax makes a pneumothorax, which becomes a tension pneumothorax under positive pressure. Lytics with no acute clot cause a major bleed, and a cardioversion with nothing to convert still needs sedation. Each comes with a reflex response scaled to the patient's reserve, so a healthy patient gets worse and a fragile one can arrest. The right procedure treats the complication.

Code mode: When the circulation stops, a timed code starts. Its clock runs in real time: the speed slider doesn't change it, and Pause stops it. The rhythm comes from the cause. A failing or ischemic LV, or a collapse in AS or HOCM, leaves VF or pulseless VT. Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, PE, hypovolemia, lost tone and the RV spiral leave PEA. A slow rate or hypoxia leaves a slow PEA. Without effective compressions, VF and PEA run down to asystole as no-flow time grows. High-quality CPR gives about 28% of the patient's resting output, an ETCO₂ in the teens and a coronary perfusion pressure (aortic minus right atrial diastolic) near 20 mmHg. Epinephrine adds about 10 mmHg that fades over a few minutes. An untreated tamponade or tension pneumothorax lets only about a third of that through, and PE or hypovolemia about half. CPP collapses within seconds of a pause, and after a pause over 10 s it builds back more slowly. ROSC is deterministic: the cause has to be treated, the last cycle needs at least 90 s of compressions with a mean CPP of 15 mmHg plus 1 for every 30 s of no flow, and the rhythm needs its drugs. VF from a sick LV needs epinephrine and an antiarrhythmic on board before a shock holds. After ROSC the model returns in post-arrest physiology: stunned ventricles that recover as coronary flow returns, high lactate, lower tone, and norepinephrine if MAP needs it. All algorithm timings and drug doses sit in one table in the code (ACLS). They follow the 2025 AHA adult cardiac arrest algorithm.

Fast forward: A rhythm and pulse check pressed partway through a cycle first finishes the 2 min cycle with compressions going. Fast forward 30 s moves the code on by 30 s, or to the 2 min mark if that comes sooner while compressions are going. Either way every second is still simulated, so CPP, ETCO₂, the epinephrine effect wearing off, lactate, no-flow time, rhythm drift and the ECPR timers come out the same as in real time. If hands are off, that time counts as a pause.

ECPR: The code panel has an ECPR group. Its selection checks update live: a witnessed arrest, CPR within 5 min, VF, pulseless VT or PEA (a rhythm that has run down to asystole doesn't qualify), refractoriness (3 shocks or 10 min of ACLS), ETCO₂ above 10 on CPR, and a projected flow time inside 60 min. Activating it starts about 10 min for the team to arrive and 15 min to cannulate, with CPR going the whole time. ECMO flow ends the code, and the debrief scores the decision and the timing. The circuit itself isn't simulated. The criteria and timings sit in one table in the code (ECPR). They follow the 2021 ELSO interim guideline, and refractoriness at 3 shocks follows the ARREST trial. Every center sets its own criteria.

Post-ROSC care: After ROSC a card tracks the post-cardiac arrest bundle on the code's real-time clock: MAP of at least 65, a definitive airway, SpO₂ 90–98% without hyperoxia, PaCO₂ 35–45, temperature control, and a 12-lead ECG with a cath decision. Without temperature control a fever builds and oxygen demand rises about 10% per degree. PCI for a STEMI gives back most of the LV contractility the infarct took. PaCO₂ is tracked on the card only; the model has no CO₂. If MAP stays under 65 for 90 s, the patient re-arrests and a new code starts. These targets sit in one table in the code (POST). They follow the 2025 AHA post-cardiac arrest guideline.

Coronary supply is a simple margin, not a perfusion model. For the RV it's MAP − RAP, less a penalty for high PA systolic pressure (which squeezes off systolic coronary flow), for tachycardia and for overstretch. For the LV it's an estimated diastolic pressure minus LVEDP, less a penalty for a high systolic load and a fast rate. When the margin runs short, that ventricle's contractility falls over several seconds and recovers more slowly. That feedback is what produces the RV spiral. Lung water builds once LA transmural pressure passes the patient's edema threshold (higher in chronic heart failure, where the lymphatics adapt), clears slowly, and lowers SaO₂. Alveolar hypoxia raises PVR.

Chronic baselines change the healthy heart the way years of disease would, including the compensation: more blood volume, more tone, hypertrophy or dilation. An acute insult is applied relative to that patient, and a hypertrophied or failing ventricle has less catecholamine reserve. The parameter values are tuned to show direction and relative size. They aren't fitted to patient data.

CO = HR × SV / 1,000. MAP = CO × SVR / 80 + RAP. CaO₂ = 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂ (saturation as a fraction). DO₂ = CO × CaO₂ × 10. VTI = SV / 3.14 for a 2.0 cm LVOT. Estimated ScvO₂ = SaO₂ − VO₂ / (1.34 × Hb × CO × 10). Central and mixed venous saturations are not interchangeable. The ScvO₂ readout is a simplified estimate; it doesn't account for upper-body extraction being different from the rest of the body. The new mixed venous SvO₂ uses Fick-derived venous content including dissolved venous oxygen, with a fixed Hill approximation (P50 26.8 mmHg, exponent 2.7).

Swan model: PCWP is mean LA pressure, taken as LVEDP from the LV's diastolic curve plus any external pressure, any mitral stenosis gradient, and the MR v-wave. There's no fixed relationship between CVP and PCWP; here it comes out of how each ventricle is loaded. PVR is RV afterload, so a high PVR raises the end-systolic volume the RV is left with and the filling pressure it needs. PCWP is estimated from the rest of the model. It doesn't come from a separate LV pressure–volume loop.

Mean PAP = PCWP + CO × PVR (Wood units). PA pulse pressure ≈ SV / pulmonary arterial compliance, capped so PAD stays above PCWP. PAS = mean PAP + ⅔ pulse pressure; PAD = mean PAP − ⅓ pulse pressure. RV systolic ≈ PAS and RV end-diastolic ≈ RAP, assuming no pulmonic valve gradient. These are approximate pressures; the model doesn't draw waveforms. Pulmonary edema worsens as transmural left-sided filling pressure rises, and edema that's already there can take a while to clear after unloading.

CI = CO / BSA; SVI = SV / BSA; PVR (dyn·s/cm⁵) = 80 × (mean PAP − PCWP) / CO; SVRI = SVR × BSA; PVRI = PVR × BSA. TPG = mean PAP − PCWP; DPG = PAD − PCWP; PAPi = (PAS − PAD) / RAP. CPO = MAP × CO / 451; CPI = CPO / BSA. LVSWI = 0.0136 × (MAP − PCWP) × SVI; RVSWI = 0.0136 × (mean PAP − RAP) × SVI. MAP and HR come from systemic monitoring, not the PA distal port.

PCWP approximates mean LA pressure when it's measured correctly and there's no pulmonary vein or mitral disease. It isn't always the same as LVEDP. External pressure can raise the measured PCWP without filling the LA any more. In a real patient, LA size also depends on compliance, rhythm and chronic remodeling, so a high PCWP doesn't need a big LA. The model doesn't estimate LA volume. Its ventricular volumes and ejection fractions are teaching estimates; at the bedside they need echo, MRI or a volumetric catheter.

Demand and consumption are different things. Consumption is capped by how much oxygen the tissue can extract, so it can fall below demand. Shunting lowers effective extraction, which is how venous saturation stays high while the tissue runs up an oxygen debt. The lactate and CO₂ gap values are set by hand for each phenotype; no equation produces them. B-lines and IVC findings are simplified for teaching. The IVC alone doesn't tell you volume status or fluid responsiveness.

The critical curve uses assumed BSA 1.8 m² and an illustrative DO₂I knee of 330 mL/min/m². Treat the curve as a reference. It isn't a universal threshold, and the phenotypes don't all follow it exactly. Extraction zones: normal around 25%, compensation around 35–50%, severe extraction above 60%; extraction failure can cause shock at a low global O₂ER.

Baseline anchors: Hb 15, SaO₂ 98%, PaO₂ 95, HR 70, SV 71, CO approximately 5, CaO₂ approximately 20, DO₂ approximately 1,000, VO₂ 250, O₂ER approximately 25%, SvO₂ approximately 73%, SVR 1,200, RAP 5, MAP approximately 80, Pmsf 7.5. Computed VTI is 22.6 cm (a common teaching approximation is 20). The reservoir narrows with venoconstriction and widens with venodilation. Width tracks unstressed capacity and compliance. The area of blood stays proportional to volume, so a change in tone doesn't add or remove blood. With constriction the unstressed layer shrinks and the stressed layer grows, and the divider also moves because the width changed. The 70/30 split between unstressed and stressed volume is a teaching approximation of venous capacitance.

In the case, the observed numbers sit in their own box, apart from the model's estimates. The model can't hit every observed value without breaking its own equations. BP 82/40 gives a calculated MAP of 54. The treatment stage and the one-hour values follow the case; animation time isn't clinical time. The case isn't a treatment order for real patients.

Each drug button shows the main mechanism of one drug. Norepinephrine raises arterial and venous tone with a little inotropy. Vasopressin raises SVR and leaves PVR mostly alone. Phenylephrine raises SVR and PVR and slows the heart by reflex. Dobutamine adds contractility and rate. Milrinone adds contractility and lowers SVR and PVR. Inhaled NO lowers only PVR, by however much that patient's pulmonary vessels still respond. Esmolol lowers HR and trims contractility in both ventricles. Rate control lowers HR alone. Atropine or pacing raises HR. Cardioversion only works on new AF: it restores the atrial kick and sinus control of the rate. Real drugs have more effects than these. The intubation button combines induction (venodilation, less sympathetic tone) with positive pressure (higher intrathoracic pressure and PVR). The lower-demand button only lowers demand. The oxygen button counts any rise in saturation. Going from a PaO₂ of 100 to 500 mmHg adds only 1.2 mL/dL, all of it dissolved. Early in acute hemorrhage the Hb may not have dropped yet; this preset shows hemorrhage after the blood has diluted and equilibrated.
